# Cyber attacks in reliability units

**A Cyber-Informed Engineering study of a synthetic navigation function**

*CPS-01 report. Every number below comes from [`validation/results/`](../validation/results/)
and is regenerated by `validation/run_all.py`. All system and attack parameters are synthetic,
illustrative assumptions ([`scope.md`](scope.md)).*

## Summary

Reliability engineers describe a system with MTBF, availability and mission-capable rates.
Security engineers describe the same system with threats, incidents and controls. This project
gives the second description in the units of the first. Each cyber attack scenario is written
as an *effect* on a system function, with a frequency and a detection-and-recovery time, and
is treated mathematically as a failure mode. On a synthetic, defense-representative navigation
chain:

* **Physical failures only.** The function is available 99.980 % of the time, with a system
  MTBF of 19 870 h.
* **With four illustrative attack scenarios.** Availability falls to 99.870 %, and **system
  MTBF falls eighteenfold, to 1 087 h**. Cyber effects account for 85 % of not-mission-capable
  time.
* **A bus-flooding denial of service alone** costs 2.3 times as much availability as every
  physical failure combined.
* **Common mode.** A denial of a whole redundancy group acts exactly as *one block in series*.
  Adding bus channels divides the physical data-path risk by about 12 500 per channel, but
  leaves the attack's contribution unchanged. A third bus channel removes 0.0 % of the
  function's unavailability.
* **Two levers.** Detection and recovery shorten outages but leave system MTBF unchanged.
  Hardening and engineered controls make losses rarer. In the example, halving attack
  opportunities removes 42 % of unavailability, a design margin that makes the delay attack
  harmless 34 %, faster recovery 23.5 %, and a second mission computer 15 %.

The exact results are verified by an independent discrete-event simulation of a 24-platform
fleet over ten years, and the physical baseline agrees with CPS-02's failure-logic model to
$10^{-12}$.

## 1. Motivation

Cyber-Informed Engineering (CIE), formalised by Idaho National Laboratory and adopted in the
U.S. Department of Energy's National CIE Strategy (2022), asks engineers to treat cyber risk
with the rigour, and in the lifecycle, of physical safety and reliability. Its first
principle is *consequence-focused design*. Its working premise is that engineering teams, not
only security teams, own the consequences.

That premise fails if the two teams cannot put their numbers side by side. A reliability
analysis that reports an MTBF and a security analysis that reports a count of incidents are
not comparable, and neither answers the operator's question: *how often will the function I
need be unavailable, and what would fix it?* This project builds the common unit.

## 2. System and scope

The system is the synthetic navigation chain of CPS-02. Two embedded GNSS/INS units, each on
its own electrical bus, publish navigation data on a dual-channel data bus to a mission
computer fed from both buses. The function is *trusted navigation data reaches the mission
computer*. Failure rates are CPS-02's; repair times are added. Everything is an assumption,
and no real platform is described.

Four attack scenarios are modelled at the **effect level** only:

| scenario | effect | target | ATT&CK for ICS |
|---|---|---|---|
| bus flood | denial, transient | data bus (all channels) | T0814 Denial of Service |
| message delay or replay | 80 ms of added latency against a 50 ms deadline | data bus | T0830 Adversary-in-the-Middle |
| false navigation messages | integrity, persistent, 50 % caught at onset | data bus | T0856 Spoof Reporting Message |
| GNSS spoofing | integrity, persistent, 90 % caught at onset | navigation sources | none (RF domain) |

## 3. Method

1. **Physical baseline** (Lesson 1). Components are alternating renewal processes. The exact
   availability, failure frequency (through Birnbaum criticality), system MTBF and mean down
   time come from enumerating component states. The Fleet Reliability Dashboard's formulas
   and the public FMC / PMC / NMC definitions (GAO-03-300, GAO-23-106217) define the metrics.
2. **Effect-level scenarios** (Lesson 2). Each attack is an episode with a sustained duration,
   a detection time and a recovery time. Transient effects last $\min(D, T_d + T_r)$,
   persistent ones $T_d + T_r$, and integrity effects may be caught at onset.
3. **Translation** (Lesson 3). Each scenario is an alternating process (idle, degrade or
   deny). It gets an equivalent MTBF and MTTR, and enters the structure by knocking out its
   whole target group. Exact metrics come from enumerating the joint states of components and
   scenarios.
4. **Combined simulation** (Lesson 4). A discrete-event Monte Carlo of a fleet produces an
   outage log with causes. Random streams are keyed per process, so attacks never perturb the
   physical history.
5. **Sensitivity** (Lesson 5). Redundancy is swept against detection time, every parameter is
   moved by ±50 % (tornado), and eight mitigations are compared, each alone.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Each attack as a failure mode, and its cost

| case | 1 − A | added ÷ physical 1 − A | system MTBF (h) | MC rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| physical only | $2.0 \times 10^{-4}$ | — | 19 870 | 0.9998 |
| + bus flood | $6.6 \times 10^{-4}$ | 2.3 | 1 817 | 0.9993 |
| + delay or replay | $6.4 \times 10^{-4}$ | 2.2 | 2 855 | 0.9994 |
| + false messages | $3.5 \times 10^{-4}$ | 0.75 | 9 969 | 0.9996 |
| + GNSS spoofing | $2.5 \times 10^{-4}$ | 0.25 | 14 220 | 0.9997 |
| + all four | $1.3 \times 10^{-3}$ | 5.5 | 1 087 | 0.9987 |

As a failure-mode entry, the bus flood reads **MTBF 2 000 h, MTTR 0.93 h**. Its outages are
short and frequent: it raises unavailability 3.3-fold but cuts system MTBF elevenfold, a
change in loss frequency that availability alone hides.

### 4.2 Redundancy against a common-mode denial

| bus channels | data path lost, physical | data path lost, bus denial |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $8.0 \times 10^{-5}$ | $4.6 \times 10^{-4}$ |
| 2 | $6.4 \times 10^{-9}$ | $4.6 \times 10^{-4}$ |
| 3 | $5.1 \times 10^{-13}$ | $4.6 \times 10^{-4}$ |

The identity $A = A_{\text{physical}} \prod_s (1 - P_s(\text{deny}))$ holds exactly whenever
each scenario denies a whole group the function needs. Redundancy inside the group can only
improve the first factor.

### 4.3 The fleet view

A 24-platform fleet over ten years (2.1 million platform-hours), simulated:

| | physical only | physical + cyber |
|---|---|---|
| function-loss events | 95 | 1 885 |
| MTBF (h, dashboard formula) | 22 130 | 1 114 |
| MTTR (h) | 4.04 | 1.39 |
| availability = MC rate | 0.9998 | 0.9988 |

Every simulated metric's 95 % interval contains its exact value. The physical-only failure
frequency is known only to about ±25 % from this much fleet history, because the redundancy
works and losses are rare. This argues for system models over system-level statistics for
high-reliability functions.

### 4.4 Where mitigation matters

| mitigation | lever | unavailability removed | system MTBF × |
|---|---|---|---|
| halve attack opportunities | frequency | 42.3 % | 1.90 |
| timing margin above the delay (an engineered control) | frequency | 33.6 % | 1.48 |
| recover twice as fast | duration | 23.5 % | 1.00 |
| second mission computer | physical | 15.3 % | 1.06 |
| integrity monitor misses 10× less | frequency | 13.8 % | 1.07 |
| detect twice as fast | duration | 11.4 % | 1.00 |
| third navigation unit | physical | 0.0 % | 1.00 |
| third bus channel | physical | 0.0 % | 1.00 |

The tornado ranks the two bus attack rates as the most influential parameters. In a real
programme, threat-informed estimates of those two numbers would be the best use of analysis
effort.

## 5. Mapping to Cyber-Informed Engineering

The results speak to ten of CIE's twelve principles (Lesson 6 has the full table):

* **Engineered controls** and **design simplification**: the timing margin, and the extra
  boxes that cost FMC and buy nothing.
* **Secure information architecture** and **interdependency evaluation**: the shared bus as a
  common mode.
* **Active defense** and **planned resilience**: detection and recovery as duration levers.
* **Consequence-focused design**: the function defined first, attacks described by their
  effect on it.

Supply-chain controls and engineering information control are not modelled.

## 6. Limitations

* **Attack inputs.** Rates, durations and response times are illustrative. The *ranking* of
  mitigations depends on them. The structural results do not: the series-block identity,
  detection as a duration-only lever, and redundancy paying only at the physical single point
  of failure. Notebook 6 shows the ranking changing at a tenth of the attack rates, with the
  third bus channel's value staying at about zero.
* **Independence.** The model assumes attacks and failures are independent, and so are the
  platforms. Attacks timed to maintenance, or fleet-wide campaigns, break this; the
  simulation, not the closed form, is the tool for them.
* **Arrival process.** Attacks arrive at a constant rate and pause during an episode. This
  causes a small, documented artifact for persistent integrity scenarios.
* **Repair and readiness.** Repair resources are unlimited and there is no supply delay (no
  NMCS). Steady-state possession is continuous. The FMC / PMC / NMC mapping is a modelling
  choice, not a reporting rule.
* **Novelty.** Attack processes inside Monte Carlo reliability evaluation are established for
  power grids. The contribution claimed here is narrower ([`related-work.md`](related-work.md)).

## 7. Conclusions

Written as failure modes, cyber attacks fit into the reliability engineer's existing
machinery: the same structure function, the same renewal arguments, the same dashboard
formulas and the same readiness statuses. Once they are there, two things become visible that
neither team sees alone. **A common-mode attack makes redundancy worthless against it, and
the model proves this for any channel count.** And **the response side of security shortens
losses without making them rarer**, so availability and MTBF can tell different stories about
the same mitigation. Both are structural findings and would survive real data. The
percentages in this report would not, and are not meant to.
